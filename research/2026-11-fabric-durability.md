# Fabric durability: what fails, why, and what to use instead

_Research date: 2026-11-06. Follows the competitor sentiment report (PR #1), which found Satisfy pieces ripping, shrinking and yellowing, and some Bandit pieces wearing out fast._

## Summary

The failures customers complain about map onto four specific material choices, not bad luck:

1. **Satisfy MothTech tees are 100% organic cotton jersey (175 g) with laser-cut holes.** Cotton jersey is one of the weakest, most shrink-prone knits used in running, and every laser hole is a cut loop that can run under tension. This lines up with "shrank and got holes after 1 to 2 wears" and "white tee yellowed in under 10 wears."
2. **Satisfy and Bandit shorts are nylon with a lot of elastane (12% to 36%), held together with bonded seams.** Elastane is the first fiber to die (heat, chlorine, sweat, sunscreen), and bonded seams depend on an adhesive that weakens with heat and washing. This fits "shorts frayed after one use" and Bandit's "hem tape coming out of shorts."
3. **Fine merino without a synthetic core is fragile.** Wool knits wear through at roughly 3,000 rubs vs about 8,000 for nylon and polyester in one cited Wyzenbeek comparison. This fits "merino shirt tore on first run."
4. **Washing and storage do the rest.** Tumble drying, softener, and residual alkali finish off elastane, adhesives and whites, and brands that only say "do not tumble dry" leave the failure to the customer.

For a "luxury that lasts" brand, the practical spec is: **nylon 6,6 or high-twist filament polyester for the body, durable-grade elastane at the lowest percentage that still fits, merino only with a nylon core or Nuyarn-style filament, sewn-and-bonded (not bonded-only) seams at stress points, reinforced pocket mouths and gel loops, and pre-shrunk, anti-yellowing finishing on any whites.**

Care labels on all of these: wash cold (30 °C max), no bleach, no tumble dry.

**Why the MothTech tee fails**

- **Cotton jersey is a weak base.** Single jersey is a thin, one-face knit. Cotton is short-staple, so abrasion pulls fibers out and thins the fabric. At 175 g it is a midweight tee, not a heavy one.
- **Laser holes are pre-made defects.** In a knit, a cut loop is the start of a run. Under tension (arm swing, pulling it on, a wet heavy shirt), the edge of each perforation is where tearing starts. The "distressed" look makes real damage hard to tell from the design.
- **Cotton shrinks unless it is compacted or pre-shrunk.** Untreated cotton jersey commonly shrinks several percent in length on first wash and dry. Satisfy says "do not tumble dry," which shifts the risk to the customer. _(General textile knowledge; I did not find a source with exact figures in this pass.)_
- **White cotton yellows from sweat, body oils and finishing chemistry.** Sebum and sweat residues oxidize if not fully washed out at 30 °C. A separate, well-documented cause is phenolic yellowing: BHT antioxidant from polybag packaging reacts with nitrogen oxides in air to form a yellow nitrophenol, catalyzed by alkaline residues and most (basic) softeners. Fixes are acidic final rinse, BHT-free packaging, and avoiding humid storage ([Parkes, Fibre2Fashion](https://www.fibre2fashion.com/industry-article/3515/yellowing-of-textiles-on-storage)).

**Why the TechSilk / Justice shorts fail**

- **High elastane content.** 12% in the shell and up to 36% in liners. Elastane (spandex, polyurethane) is the most fragile fiber in the garment. It loses recovery permanently above about 60 °C, and is degraded by chlorine, UV, sweat, body oils and sunscreen ([Spandex by Yard care guide](https://spandexbyyard.com/pages/fabric-care-guide), a vendor source). Once elastane breaks, the fabric bags, the surface shows "grin-through" and edges start to fray.
- **Bonded seams and edges.** A bonded seam is a strip of hot-melt adhesive (often polyurethane) instead of thread. It is smooth and light, which is why premium brands use it, but its strength depends on the glue. Heat from dryers, repeated washing, and body oils soften or hydrolyze it, and the seam peels. Raw laser-cut bonded hems have no thread to stop fraying. _(Mechanism is standard textile engineering; I did not find a source with test numbers in this pass.)_
- **Thin, light shells.** 100 to 130 g for a whole pair of shorts is very light. Light knits have less material to abrade before they fail, especially at the inner thigh and pocket mouths.

**Where Bandit fails:** the reported failures were hem tape coming out of shorts and socks ripping within two months. Hem tape and grip tape are bonded features, so the failure matches the adhesive mechanism above. ### Merino in general

- Next-to-skin merino is 17 to 19 microns. Fine fibers feel luxurious but are delicate, and wool's tenacity is about 1.0 to 1.7 g/denier vs 4.5 to 9+ for nylon ([Absurdly Optimized review](https://www.absurdlyoptimized.com/outdoors/t-shirt/)).
- The same source cites wool knits wearing through at about 3,000 Wyzenbeek double rubs vs about 8,000 for nylon and polyester. Holes start at flex points like elbows and where a pack strap or arm rubs.
- Icebreaker removed its nylon core in its "Plastic-Free by '23" push, which is one example of a premium brand trading durability for a sustainability message.

## 2. Materials and constructions that hold up better and still feel premium

### Base fabrics

| Use | Recommended | Why | Premium feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shorts and pants shell | **Nylon 6,6 woven or tight double-knit** (e.g. CORDURA-certified lightweight, or Italian mill nylon 6,6 jerseys) | Nylon has the highest abrasion resistance of common apparel fibers; 6,6 is stronger and more heat-stable than nylon 6 | Can be sueded, matte or peached; reads as luxury, not gym |
| Leggings, liners, compression | **Nylon/elastane interlock or double-knit**, elastane kept around 15 to 20%, using a durable-grade elastane (e.g. LYCRA XTRA LIFE, which the maker says resists chlorine, UV and sunscreen better, no numbers published) | Double-knit hides elastane grin-through and spreads load; durable-grade elastane slows the first failure mode | Bandit's Stamina already shows this works |
| Tees and tanks | **Filament polyester or polyester/merino with nylon core**, mid weight (130 to 160 g) | Filament yarns don't pill or thin like short-staple cotton | Fine-gauge filament knits feel smooth and drape well |
| Merino pieces | **Core-spun merino (nylon core) or Nuyarn-style merino twisted around a filament** | Maker claims about 123,000 Martindale rubs vs 14,000 to 18,000 for traditional merino (manufacturer claim, [Echos](https://echoscomm.com/nuyarn-merino-wool-with-no-holes-heres-why/)). An independent 50+ wear test of a 95 g Nuyarn tee found only surface pilling | Keeps merino's hand and odor resistance |
| Whites | Pre-shrunk / compacted, acid-neutralized finish, BHT-free packaging | Prevents yellowing and shrink before the customer ever washes it | |

Research from PubMed (Polymers journal, University of Zagreb) adds two useful data points:

- Aging sportswear knits lose abrasion resistance over time; in that study recycled polyester held its wear resistance better after aging than the conventional polyester tested ([Salopek Čubrić et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym17121616)).
- A separate study from the same group found conventional polyester yarn kept higher abrasion resistance, smoother surface and better elasticity after aging than recycled ([Salopek Čubrić & Čubrić, 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym16121725)). Taken together: recycled vs virgin is not automatically a durability downgrade, but it depends on the specific yarn, so test the actual mill fabric.
- For elastane blends, 200 hours of chlorinated pool water cut breaking force by about 12%, and 300 hours cut it by about 65% ([Potočić Matković et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym16213050)). Failure is not gradual; it falls off a cliff. That is a strong argument for durable-grade elastane and for lower elastane percentages.

### Construction details that matter more than fiber

- **Seams:** use flatlock or coverstitch seams at the crotch, inseam and pocket mouths, and reserve bonding for low-stress areas or use **bonded plus stitched** at stress points. Flatlock seams were specifically praised as holding up in a Bandit half-tights review.
- **Pockets and gel loops:** these carry the most load and abrasion (phone weight, repeated insertion). Bartack the corners, add a reinforcing tape or a second layer of nylon 6,6 at pocket mouths, and stitch gel loops rather than bonding them. This is Tut's core feature, so it should be the most over-built part of the garment.
- **Hems:** stitched or folded-and-stitched hems on shorts; if a raw laser-cut hem is used for looks, back it with a stitched tape.
- **Perforations:** if we want ventilation, use knitted-in mesh zones (eyelet or pique structures) instead of laser-cut holes, so there are no cut loops to run.
- **Weight:** avoid the ultralight race-day extreme for everyday pieces. A little more weight in the shell is cheap durability.

### Care and testing

- Put care instructions on the hangtag that match how people really wash (many tumble dry). Better still, choose fabrics that survive a low tumble dry, so the warranty does not depend on perfect care.
- Ask mills for test data before choosing: Martindale abrasion (ISO 12947), pilling (ISO 12945 or ASTM D3512), dimensional stability after washing (ISO 6330 / 5077), seam strength, and colorfastness to sweat and light. Comparing these numbers across sample fabrics is how we back up a "lasts" promise with evidence.
- Do a wear test of finalist samples (e.g. 30 runs and 30 washes, including tumble drying) and photograph the results. That evidence can become marketing content too.

## 3. Was PubMed worth it?

**Partly.** PubMed's main value was that it indexes the open-access journal _Polymers_ through PubMed Central, which surfaced three real sportswear aging and abrasion studies from the University of Zagreb's textile faculty, plus a 2025 study tying sweat odor on polyester sportswear to _Staphylococcus hominis_ ([Yan et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12088-025-01520-w)). It also covers skin-side topics such as chafing, moisture and friction (e.g. [Romanelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.12968/jowc.2023.32.7.411) recommending moisture-wicking textiles in skin folds).

But most fabric-durability science (abrasion testing, seam bonding, yarn engineering) is in textile and engineering journals like _Textile Research Journal_ and _Journal of the Textile Institute_, which PubMed does not index. For this topic, PubMed is a useful supplement, not the main source. It will be more useful later for skin, chafing, odor and comfort claims.
