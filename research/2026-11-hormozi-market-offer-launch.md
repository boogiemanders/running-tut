# What to research next, and what we found (Hormozi lens)

Researched 2026-11-06 for Running Tut. Builds on `research/2026-11-competitor-sentiment.md` (PR #1). Design and aesthetics are covered in a separate thread and are left out here.

## Why these questions

Alex Hormozi's frameworks each ask something the competitor report doesn't answer yet:

| Framework | The question it forces | Status before today |
|---|---|---|
| **Market selection**: pain, purchasing power, easy to target, growing | *Who exactly do we sell to first?* | Not researched. "Runners who want style" is too broad. |
| **Value equation**: (dream outcome × perceived likelihood) ÷ (time delay × effort) | *What makes a $200 pant feel like a no-brainer?* | Partly. We know the dream (look good, carry everything, it lasts), not how to raise belief in it. |
| **Grand Slam Offer**: price high, stack bonuses, strong guarantee, scarcity, name it | *What do we promise that Satisfy and Bandit don't?* | Not researched. Competitor guarantees and price ladder unknown. |
| **Lead gen (Core Four)**: warm outreach, content, cold outreach, paid ads | *How did peer brands get their first 1,000 customers?* | Not researched. |
| **Unit economics**: LTV:CAC, "get paid to acquire customers" | *Can we afford to acquire a customer?* | Not researched. |

So this round covers five things: the starting customer, the price ladder, guarantees, how peers launched, and the numbers.

## Read this first: how good is the evidence?

- Growth and spending figures come from trade press (Fitt Insider, Athletech News) and race organisers. Some Strava figures are from Sept 2025, over a year old.
- Unit-cost and CAC figures come mostly from factory and agency marketing pages. They disagree widely, so treat them as ranges, not facts.
- Brand launch stories come from interviews and press (2019 to 2025), not company filings.
- Tracksmith's own site refused automated reads, so its return policy isn't confirmed.

## 1. Who to sell to first (market selection)

**Recommended starting customer: the urban run-club runner training for a half or full marathon**, roughly 25 to 45, who runs to and from work or a club and already spends on gear.

How that customer scores on Hormozi's four tests:

- **Pain: strong.** Long runs mean carrying a phone, keys, cards and 3 to 6 gels. The earlier report found runners resorting to belts and tied jackets, and storage is Bandit's number one request every season. Run-commuters also need trousers they can wear into a coffee shop or office.
- **Purchasing power: strong.** US running apparel sales rose about 10% to nearly $1B; $150+ shoe sales rose 46% (Circana, via Fitt, Aug 2026). Premium runners spend $3,500 to $5,000 a year on clothing alone (Bloomberg, via Athletech, Aug 2026). The average runner spends just under $1,000 a year on the sport, with women spending about 21% more than men (aggregated by Ringly, unverified).
- **Easy to target: very strong.** Run clubs are the distribution channel. Run clubs on Strava grew about 4x in the US in 2025 and running crews make up 39% of all Strava groups (Fitt, Sep 2025). Marathons are a calendar of gatherings: NYC had 240,000+ lottery applicants for 2026 (about 1% admitted), London 1.13M ballot applications, up 36% (Running Magazine, Mar 2026; Marathon Handbook, May 2025). Every major marathon has an expo and a shakeout-run scene where brands do pop-ups.
- **Growing: strong.** Road-race finishers rose 17.1% in 2024, half marathons 20.9% (Road Race Management, via Ringly). Satisfy expects 50 to 60% growth in 2026 and District Vision about 30% a year (Bloomberg, via Fitt).

**Gender note:** runners are now roughly 50/50 men and women (London 2026 ballot: 49.9% men, 49.6% women). The earlier report found women complain loudest about missing pockets and some buy men's gear for that reason. A women's pant with real storage is a sharp wedge, not a side line.

**Not first:** trail and ultra runners (they already carry vests, and Patagonia, Salomon and others own the space) and casual joggers (low spend, hard to target).

## 2. The price ladder for running pants

Full-length running pants today (Fleet Feet buyer's guides, 2026):

| Pant | Price | Storage |
|---|---|---|
| Craft Core Dry Active (W) | $55 | none mentioned |
| Brooks Spartan 2.0 (M) | $80 | zip hand pockets |
| rabbit EZ Joggers 2.0 (W) | $88 | one front zip, fits most phones |
| rabbit Runners (M) | $100 | one back zip |
| Nike Swift (W) | $105 | drop-in sides + back zip |
| Vuori Performance Jogger | $110 | side pockets + thigh zip (M) |
| Brooks Momentum 2.0 (W) | $120 | two zip pockets |
| lululemon Pace Breaker (M) | $128 | one phone pocket |
| Tracksmith Session Pant (M) | $130 | two hand + one zip |

Above that sits Satisfy (shorts around $220, tops $140 to $320) with no comparable storage-led pant found.

**Takeaway:** the mainstream ceiling for a running pant is about $130, and none of the best-rated ones carry more than three pockets. A **$180 to $220** pant with genuinely phone-sized, bounce-free storage plus designer finish sits in an empty slot between Tracksmith and Satisfy. Hormozi's advice is to price high and justify it with the offer, not to undercut. At $130 you are competing with Tracksmith and lululemon on their terms.

## 3. Guarantees: what competitors promise

| Brand | Return window | Durability promise |
|---|---|---|
| **Satisfy** | 14 days, unworn, tags on; fee on collabs | **None stated.** Defects handled case by case by email (the earlier report found coupons offered instead of refunds). |
| **Bandit** | 21 days, unworn, unwashed; international orders final sale | **None stated.** |
| **Janji** | (standard) | **5-year "Run Everywhere Guarantee"**: repair, replace or store credit if it "rips, tears, or breaks... due to any defect." Excludes normal wear. |
| **Patagonia** | No time limit, current and past seasons | **Ironclad, lifetime**: repair, replace or refund. Wear and tear repaired, free in the UK/EU currently (4 to 6 weeks). |
| **Fleet Feet** (retailer) | 30-day full refund, worn | n/a |
| **Tracksmith** | Not confirmed (site refused automated reads) | Not confirmed |

**Takeaway:** the two brands Running Tut is positioned between (Satisfy and Bandit) offer **no durability guarantee at all**, and only accept unworn returns. That is the single biggest offer gap found. "Luxury that lasts" becomes believable, not just a slogan, if it's backed by a written promise.

## 4. A Grand Slam Offer to test

Built from the gaps above. Every item answers a specific complaint from the competitor report.

- **Core product:** the first pant (working name: "the Carry-All Pant"), with phone-sized, anti-bounce pockets reachable by feel, plus gel loops.
- **Guarantee (raises perceived likelihood):** "Run it for 5 years. If a seam, zip or pocket fails, we repair or replace it, free." This matches Janji's 5 years but, unlike Satisfy and Bandit, covers worn product. Add a **"run in it for 30 days" fit promise** (worn returns or exchange), which removes the risk of buying a premium pant online.
- **Bonuses (reduce effort):** free first repair or hem, a real human support line (Satisfy's "NO PHONE number" complaint), free size exchange both ways.
- **Scarcity and urgency:** a numbered first run, founding-member pricing for preorders only.
- **Proof (raises perceived likelihood):** publish a wear test (e.g. "1,000 km and 50 washes") with photos, since the leading complaint about Satisfy is shrinking, holes and yellowing within a few wears.

The guarantee has a cost. It only works if the product really is durable, so it should be priced into the margin (see section 6) and stress-tested on samples before launch.

## 5. How peer brands got their first customers (lead gen)

**Bandit (Brooklyn, 2020).**
- First product was a **running sock**, chosen as a cheap "trust-building product" with "great margins" (co-founder Nick West). Tested on friends and colleagues; early drops sold out by word of mouth.
- **No paid ads for the first year.** Growth came from free community runs: 50+ people at the first Saturday run, later 100+ weekly from the Williamsburg store.
- 10+ pop-ups in the 12 months before NYC Marathon, each with revenue and lead goals, tracking the knock-on effect on online sales.
- Sources: Gear Patrol (Jul 2023), Shopify (Oct 2023).

**Tracksmith (Boston, 2014).**
- Grassroots first. "Paid marketing is just an opportunity to layer on top of the foundation" (co-founder Matt Taylor). Paid ads (Instagram, search) only began around year four.
- Its own club (Hare A.C.), free runs three days a week at its Boston store, pop-ups at the Boston, London, Chicago and NYC marathons, and brand films.
- Doubled each year 2017 to 2019 on $6.7M raised; sales up 280% from 2019 to 2022.
- Sources: Digiday (Sep 2019), The Case for Brand (Nov 2023).

**Others.**
- rabbit launched on Kickstarter and was funded in under a day.
- Endorphins (NYC) grew from a 2022 training group to 25,000+ runners.
- Sources: Shopify/Stacker (Jul 2025).

**Takeaway, mapped to Hormozi's Core Four:**
1. **Warm outreach first.** Anders and Tut's own running circles, then 2 to 3 local run clubs. Give wear-test samples to club leads.
2. **Content.** The wear test and "what fits in the pockets" videos are content that is also proof.
3. **A low-cost "trust" product**, Bandit-style, could come before the pant (e.g. a gel-loop belt or socks) to build a list cheaply. Optional.
4. **Paid ads last**, once the offer converts organically. Every peer brand that succeeded waited.
5. **Preorders** (own site or Kickstarter) to fund the first run and prove demand before paying factory minimums.

## 6. The numbers (unit economics)

**Making it (factory and marketplace pages, less reliable):**
- Full custom MOQs: 300 to 500 pieces per style per colour; custom fabric about 500 pieces or 300 to 500 kg per colour.
- Smaller cut-and-sew (US, Maker's Row): 50 to 100 per style, $2,000 to $10,000 minimum order value.
- Sampling: about $75 to $150 per prototype, plus $500 to $1,000 pattern development per style (China factory).
- First production run about 30 to 45 days after sample approval.
- Basic running shorts quoted at $12 to $30 a unit. A premium pant with technical fabric and zips will cost much more; real quotes are needed.

**Selling it (agency benchmarks, 2026, wide disagreement):**

| Metric | Range |
|---|---|
| Apparel CAC, blended | $30 to $95 |
| Luxury CAC | $120 to $400+ |
| Gross margin | 55 to 75% |
| Site conversion | 2 to 3.5% |
| 12-month repeat rate | 15 to 30% |
| Apparel return rate | 15 to 40% |
| Premium apparel average order value | $150 to $200 |

**Rough first-order math for a $200 pant** (illustrative; the landed cost is an assumption until we have quotes):

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Price | $200 |
| Landed cost (assumed) | −$50 |
| Shipping, payment fees, returns, guarantee reserve (assumed) | −$30 |
| **Left before marketing** | **≈ $120** |

At luxury CAC ($120 to $400) paid ads lose money on the first order. That matches what every peer brand did: **community and preorders first, ads later.** It also means a second product (shorts, a top) matters early, because profit comes from the repeat order. Hormozi's rule of thumb is LTV at least 3x CAC.

## What we still need (can't be done from a desk)

1. **10 to 15 customer conversations** with run-club marathon trainees: what do you carry, what do you run in now, what would you pay, what made you stop wearing something. This tests sections 1, 2 and 4 directly.
2. **Factory quotes** for one pant in two fabrics at 100, 300 and 500 units, from 2 to 3 factories (Portugal, Vietnam or Taiwan for premium technical). This replaces the assumed $50 landed cost.
3. **A pocket fit test** with current phones (iPhone Pro Max size) and 4 to 6 gels on a 90-minute run, against Bandit and Tracksmith pants.
4. **A preorder smoke test**: a landing page with the offer from section 4, sent to warm contacts only, to measure real intent before paying minimums.
